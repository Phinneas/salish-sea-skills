# Greenwashing Check — Keurig Dr Pepper K-Cup recyclability claims

**Audited:** 2026-08-29 | **Claims found:** 2 | **High risk:** 1

## Claim-by-claim audit

### Claim 1: "100% recyclable" (unqualified K-Cup pod claim, live since end of 2020; underlying testing representation made to investors in 2019/2020 Form 10-K filings)

- **Type:** Recyclable claim, § 260.12
- **Substantiation status:** Unsubstantiated
- **Risk:** High
- **Why:** § 260.12(a) permits an unqualified recyclable claim only when
  recycling facilities are available to a substantial majority (≥60%) of
  consumers/communities where the item is sold. § 260.12(d) goes further:
  if any component — including shape or size — significantly limits the
  item's actual recyclability, an unqualified recyclable claim is
  deceptive even if the underlying material is technically recyclable.
  Here, the pod is made of real #5 polypropylene (a recyclable material),
  but per the SEC's finding, two of the largest U.S. recycling companies
  — operating more than a third of the country's facilities — had given
  "significant negative feedback" on the pods and did not intend to
  accept them, information Keurig had in hand from its own testing but
  did not disclose. That is a direct match to § 260.12(d)'s own example
  language: "An item that is made from recyclable material, but, because
  of its shape, size, or some other attribute, is not accepted in
  recycling programs, should not be marketed as recyclable." The same
  reasoning was later applied by a federal court to Colgate's
  "Recyclable" toothpaste tube claim on nearly identical facts (tube made
  of recyclable material, but shape/residue meant it wasn't actually
  processed). This is not a marginal or novel interpretation — it is the
  core, litigated meaning of § 260.12(d), and it produced a $1.5 million
  SEC penalty for the omission alone (SEC Release No. 34-100983, Sept.
  10, 2024).
- **Fix:** Don't use an unqualified "recyclable" claim for a single-use
  pod unless recycling facilities actually accept it in practice for a
  substantial majority of the markets where it's sold. If that isn't
  true, qualify the claim to name the real state of facts — see Claim 2
  below for the direction Keurig itself moved the language after the
  settlement, with one remaining gap noted.

### Claim 2: "Our K-Cup pods are made from recyclable polypropylene plastic (also known as #5 plastic), which is widely accepted in curbside recycling systems across North America. We continue to encourage consumers to check with their local recycling program to verify acceptance of pods, as they are not recycled in many communities." (Keurig's public statement following the September 2024 settlement)

- **Type:** Recyclable claim, § 260.12, with qualification per § 260.3
- **Substantiation status:** Unclear
- **Risk:** Medium
- **Why:** This is a real improvement over Claim 1 — it specifies the
  material (#5 polypropylene, a verifiable fact) rather than asserting
  the finished product is broadly recyclable, and it now discloses,
  clearly and in the same statement, that the pods "are not recycled in
  many communities" — the exact disclosure § 260.12(d) and the earlier
  omission were missing. Two things still keep this from a clean
  Low/Substantiated rating: (1) "widely accepted" is itself an
  unsubstantiated quantitative claim dressed as a qualifier — the Green
  Guides' own substantiation standard (§ 260.2) requires competent and
  reliable evidence for *every* claim, including qualifying language, and
  "widely" isn't tied to a percentage, a source, or a defined geography
  the way § 260.12(a)'s 60%-of-consumers test would require to actually
  support an unqualified-style claim; (2) the statement doesn't disclose
  the specific fact that drove the SEC's finding — that two major
  recycling operators representing over a third of U.S. facilities have
  said they don't intend to accept the pods at all, which is materially
  more specific and more negative than "not recycled in many
  communities." A consumer reading "widely accepted... check locally" is
  likely to interpret their own odds as better than that fact supports.
- **Fix:** Replace "widely accepted" with a sourced figure or drop the
  characterization entirely and lead with the actionable instruction
  ("Check with your local curbside program before recycling — many
  programs, including [name known non-accepting operators/regions if
  disclosable], do not currently accept K-Cup pods"). Naming the real
  gap plainly is what moves this from Medium back to Low risk.

## Highest-priority fixes

The unqualified "100% recyclable" claim (Claim 1) is the one that
produced real regulatory consequences — a $1.5M SEC penalty — because the
company had internal data (two major recyclers' refusal to accept the
product) contradicting the claim and didn't disclose it. The lesson for
any organization running this check on its own copy: an unqualified
recyclability claim needs facility-acceptance evidence behind it before
it ships, not after a regulator asks for it. The post-settlement language
(Claim 2) shows the right direction — name the material, disclose the
real limitation — but "widely accepted" still needs a number or a source
before it clears a Low-risk rating.
