# Logic Model: Cascade Watershed Alliance Youth Stream Monitoring Program

**Built:** 2026-08-28 · **For opportunity:** Environmental Education Local Grants Program (EPA Region 10)

## Impact statement (7-10 years)
A community whose local waterways are measurably healthier, sustained by a
generation of residents — starting with the students in this program — who
understand watershed health well enough to act on it and who pass that
practice on to others.

## The model

| Resources/Inputs | Activities | Outputs | Short-term Outcomes (1-3 yrs) | Longer-term Outcomes (4-6 yrs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EPA award ($60,000) + $15,000 cash match + $5,000 in-kind (partner land trust equipment access, school classroom space) | Recruit and train students at 3 partner schools in EPA-approved stream monitoring protocols | 90 students trained across 3 schools (target, based on prior-year enrollment at each partner school) | Students demonstrate increased water-quality literacy on a post-program assessment, scored against the pre-program baseline | Trained students who continue past one program year become peer mentors for incoming cohorts, sustaining the training pipeline without proportional new staff time |
| 2 part-time staff (Program Coordinator, Education Coordinator) + volunteer stream-monitoring lead | Conduct monthly water-quality monitoring (pH, turbidity, temperature, macroinvertebrate counts) at designated sites with trained students | 12 monitoring events completed; a full year of monthly data points logged at each site | Watershed council receives a full year of student-collected data points it did not previously have, filling a monitoring gap the council has identified in prior planning documents | Multi-year accumulated dataset lets the watershed council and partner land trust identify trend lines (not just single-year snapshots) at monitored sites, informing where restoration investment is targeted |
| 3 partner schools (existing relationships) | Deliver classroom curriculum connecting monitoring data to watershed science, tied to the "Community Projects" educational priority | Curriculum delivered in all 3 partner classrooms; each classroom completes at least one data-to-action project | Students report increased personal stewardship actions (e.g. reducing household runoff contributors) on a post-program self-report survey | Partner schools institutionalize the curriculum into their own science programming, continuing after this specific grant period ends |
| Land trust field sites + existing monitoring equipment, supplemented by new EPA-funded equipment | Host 4 quarterly stream restoration workdays (invasive removal, native planting) at monitored sites | 4 workdays completed; ~150 volunteer-hours per workday (600 total), roughly half from student participants and half from broader community volunteers | — (restoration outputs feed the longer-term water-quality outcome directly; no meaningful short-term water-quality change is claimed from one year of workdays) | Accumulated restoration work (this grant period plus subsequent years) produces a measurable improvement in a specific water-quality indicator (e.g. streambank stability, reduced sedimentation) at the monitored sites — because sustained riparian restoration is the established mechanism for that specific indicator, not because of the monitoring alone |
| $15,000 of EPA funds structured as 3 subawards (≤$5,000 each) to school environmental clubs / the partner land trust for site-specific micro-projects | Manage subaward selection, execution, and reporting for the 3 micro-projects | 3 subawards distributed and completed; each micro-project delivers a defined local deliverable (e.g. a native plant buffer at one school site) | Subrecipients complete their micro-projects on time and within scope, demonstrated by the org's first subaward-management track record | A functioning subaward-management track record makes the org a credible applicant for larger, subaward-bearing awards in future cycles |
| Full program dataset + partner relationships | Host an end-of-year community data showcase presenting student findings | 1 showcase event; target 75+ community attendees including families and local officials | Increased community awareness of local water-quality issues, evidenced by showcase attendance and any resulting local media or council follow-up | Showcase becomes an annual community fixture, expanding the program's stewardship reach beyond the students directly enrolled |

## The if-then chain (stated explicitly)
1. If we have the EPA award plus matching cash and in-kind support, 2
   part-time staff, and existing school and land trust relationships, then
   we can run monthly monitoring, quarterly restoration workdays, classroom
   curriculum, and 3 managed subawards.
2. If we run those activities, then we produce 90 trained students, a full
   year of monitoring data, 4 completed restoration workdays, and 3
   completed subaward micro-projects.
3. If those outputs are achieved, then participants experience increased
   water-quality literacy and early stewardship behavior — because
   hands-on data collection tied directly to a curriculum unit is the
   mechanism the literacy assessment is designed to measure, not a
   generic exposure effect.
4. If those short-term outcomes hold across multiple cohorts, then over
   time trained students become peer mentors, partner schools
   institutionalize the curriculum, and the accumulated multi-year dataset
   and restoration work begin to shift measurable water-quality
   indicators — because riparian restoration's effect on indicators like
   sedimentation is a multi-year, cumulative mechanism, not a one-workday
   effect.
5. If those longer-term outcomes are sustained past this single award,
   they contribute to a community with measurably healthier waterways and
   a self-renewing culture of watershed stewardship.

## Weak links flagged
- **Step 3's stewardship-behavior claim relies on a self-report survey**,
  not observed behavior. Self-reported "I reduced household runoff
  contributors" is weaker evidence than the literacy assessment's
  pre/post design. If a reviewer presses on this, the honest answer is
  that behavior change is claimed as self-reported intent in year one,
  with observed behavior change deferred to the longer-term outcomes
  where the org has more time to build a real measurement approach.
- **The water-quality-indicator improvement in the longer-term column
  assumes restoration work continues past this single award period.** One
  year of 4 quarterly workdays is unlikely, on its own, to move a
  measurable indicator — the model is honest that this outcome depends on
  sustained multi-year investment this specific grant only partially
  funds, not on this award alone.
- **The subaward-management outcome assumes the org's first attempt at
  subaward administration goes well enough to be a track record worth
  citing.** This is a real, not cosmetic, execution risk the fit
  assessment already flagged — the logic model doesn't resolve that risk,
  it just makes clear where it sits in the causal chain.
- **The curriculum-institutionalization outcome depends on partner
  schools' own budget and staffing decisions**, which are outside the
  org's control — worth naming as an assumption rather than a guarantee,
  particularly if a reviewer asks what happens if a partner school's
  staffing changes.
